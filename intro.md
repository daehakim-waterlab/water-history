*Panta rhei* — "everything flows." The phrase is attached to the Greek philosopher Heraclitus, and it is a good motto for this book. Water and air are the most familiar things in the world, yet the science of how they move took more than two thousand years to build, and it is still unfinished. Along the way, the study of flow shaped how people thought about the heavens, about energy, about machines and war, and about the water that sustains cities and farms.

This short web textbook tells that story for students of civil engineering, hydraulics and hydrology. It is not a replacement for a textbook of fluid mechanics. Instead, it answers questions that a textbook usually leaves out: *Who* first wrote down the equations you use, *why* they were interested in the problem, and *what was happening around them* when they did it.

## Why read a history of fluid mechanics?

**Equations have authors and contexts.** Bernoulli's equation, the Navier–Stokes equations, the Reynolds number, Darcy's law and the unit hydrograph were each the answer to a practical or philosophical problem of their time: supplying water to a city, aiming a cannon, building a faster ship, flying, or forecasting the weather. Knowing the problem makes the equation easier to understand and to remember.

**Theory and practice have often been apart.** For two centuries, mathematicians produced an elegant "hydrodynamics" that could not predict the drag on a ship, while engineers built a practical "hydraulics" from experiments and empirical coefficients. Much of the history in this book is the story of how the two were finally reconnected, through the concepts of similarity, viscosity, the boundary layer and turbulence.

**Science and society flow together.** Fluid mechanics grew up alongside revolutions in astronomy, politics and industry. The word *revolution* itself began as a term for the turning of the heavens. Steam engines, artillery, telegraph cables, aircraft, rockets and computers all fed problems into the science of flow, and the science fed answers back. Wars and emigrations moved people and ideas from one country to another.

**Water science is part of the same story.** Hydrology grew from the same roots: the measurement of rainfall and river flow, the water balance, and the physics of evaporation and seepage. The last chapter traces that history up to the present.

## A note on sources

The narrative draws on standard histories of hydraulics, hydrodynamics, aerodynamics and hydrology, listed at the end of each chapter. Its approach of reading the history of fluid mechanics alongside political, industrial and intellectual revolutions owes much to Min Tae-gi's *Panta Rhei* (판타 레이: 혁명과 낭만의 유체 과학사, ScienceBooks, 2022), a Korean book that tells the history of fluid science from Leonardo da Vinci's vortex drawings to modern rocketry. Readers of Korean are encouraged to read it alongside this text.

Dates are given in the Common Era unless marked BCE. Where historians disagree about a date or an attribution, the text says so.
